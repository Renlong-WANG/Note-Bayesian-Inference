\chapter{Bayesian Filter}

\section{Basic Settings}

Let 
\[
    \bm{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\bm{x}}}
\]
denote the state vector at time $t$, which represents the unknow quantity we want to infer.

At each time $t$, we observe 
\[
    \bm{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\bm{z}}}.
\]
The observation contains information about the hidden state $\bm{x}_t$, but may be noisy and uncertain. For convenience, deine the observation history up to time $t$ as
\[
    \bm{z}_{1:t} = \left\{ \bm{z}_1, \dots, \bm{z}_t \right\}
\]

Similarly, suppose that a control input is available at each time $t$, denoted by
\[
    \bm{u}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\bm{u}}},
\]
with the corresponding control history 
\[
    \bm{u}_{1:t} = \left\{ \bm{u}_1, \dots, \bm{u}_t \right\}.
\] 

Therefore, the available information evolves sequentially as
\[
    \bm{u}_1, \bm{z}_1, \bm{u}_2, \bm{z}_2, \bm{u}_3, \bm{z}_3, \dots
\]
whereas the states
\[
    \bm{x}_1, \bm{x}_2, \dots.
\]
are not directly observed.

Since subsequent inference depends on the state, the objective is to characterize the uncertainty associated with the random variable $\bm{x}_t$ given all available information. This uncertainty is represented by the belief state
\[
    \mathrm{bel}(\bm{x}_t) = p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t}, \bm{u}_{1:t}).
\]
The belief state is therefore a probability distribution over the possible values of $\bm{x}_t$ conditioned on the available observation and control histories. It is also commonly referred to as the posterior state distribution, information state, or state of knowledge. 


\section{Core Formula of Bayesian Filter}

The key idea of the Bayesian filter is to represent uncertainty explicitly using the calculas of probability theory. 

At each time step, the hidden state $\bm{x}_t$ evolves from the previous state $\bm{x}_{t-1}$ under the control input $\bm{u}_t$, while the observation $\bm{z}_t$ is generated from the corresponding state $\bm{x}_t$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig-1}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{fig-1.png}
    \caption{Graphical representation of the Bayesian filtering model}
    \label{fig-1}
\end{figure}

However, the belief, $p(\text{current sate} | \text{all past information})$, is intractable. This leads to the Markov assumption: future state conditionally independent of past control inputs and observations given present state. We therefore have
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        & p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{x}_{t-1}, \bm{u}_t, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t-1}) = p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{x}_{t-1}, \bm{u}_t), \\
        & p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_{t}, \bm{u}_t, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t-1}) = p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_{t}).
    \end{aligned}
\]

Using Bayes' rule and the Markov assumptions,
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{bel}(\bm{x}_t) & = p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) = p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{z}_t, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \leftarrow \text{Definition of belief} \\
        & = \frac{p(\bm{x}_t, \bm{z}_t |\bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t})}{p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t-1},\bm{u}_{1:t})} \leftarrow \text{Condition probability} \\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{x}_t, \bm{z}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \leftarrow \text{Normalization constant}\\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) p(\bm{x}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \leftarrow \text{Bayes rule}  \\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t) p(\bm{x}_t |\bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \leftarrow \text{Markov assumption}  \\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t) \int p(\bm{x}_t, \bm{x}_{t-1} | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1} \leftarrow \text{Marginalization} \\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t) \int p(\bm{x}_t  | \bm{x}_{t-1}, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) p (\bm{x}_{t-1} | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1} \leftarrow \text{Probability chain rule} \\
        & = \eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t) \int p(\bm{x}_t  | \bm{x}_{t-1}, \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) p (\bm{x}_{t-1} | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t-1}) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1} \leftarrow \text{Unnecessary condition} \\
        & = \underbrace{\eta_t p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_t)}_{\text{correction}}  \underbrace{\int p(\bm{x}_t  | \bm{x}_{t-1}, \bm{u}_t) \mathrm{bel}(\bm{x}_{t-1}) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1}}_{\text{prediction}}
    \end{aligned} 
\]
where $\eta_t$ is a normalization constant,
\[
    \eta_t^{-1} = p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) = \int p(\bm{z}_t | \bm{x}_{t}) p(\bm{x}_{t} | \bm{z}_{1:t-1}, \bm{u}_{1:t}) \mathrm{d} \bm{x}_t
\]
and $\mathrm{bel}(x_0)$ = $p(\bm{x}_0)$.

The Bayesian filter therefore provides a recursive update approach to calculate belief tractably consisting of two steps: prediction, which propagates the previous belief through the state transition model, and correction, which incorporates the latest observation through the observation model.

\section{Kalman Filter}

The Kalman filter is a special case of the Bayesian filter in which the state transition and observation models are linear and all uncertainties are Gaussian.

\subsection{Prediction Step}

Suppose that the state transition is linear in the previous state $\bm{x}_{t-1}$ and control input $\bm{u}_t$:
\[
    \bm{x}_t
    =
    \mathbf{F}_{t}\bm{x}_{t-1}
    +
    \mathbf{B}_{t}\bm{u}_{t}
    +
    \bm{q}_t,
    \qquad
    \bm{q}_t
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{Q}_t),
    \qquad
    t\in\{1,\dots,T\}.
\]
Assume that the process noise $\bm{q}_t$ is independent of the previous state. Then
\[
    p(\bm{x}_t\mid\bm{x}_{t-1},\bm{u}_t)
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \mathbf{F}_t\bm{x}_{t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t,
        \mathbf{Q}_t
    \right).
\]

Suppose that the posterior state distribution at time $t-1$ is Gaussian:
\[
    p(
        \bm{x}_{t-1}
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t-1}
    )
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \bm{\mu}_{t-1\mid t-1},
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1\mid t-1}
    \right),
\]
where $\bm{\mu}_{t-1\mid t-1}$ and $\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1\mid t-1}$ denote the posterior mean and covariance based on all information available up to time $t-1$.

The prediction step of the Bayesian filter is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    p(
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t}
    )
    &=
    \int
    p(
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{x}_{t-1},
        \bm{u}_t
    )
    p(
        \bm{x}_{t-1}
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t-1}
    )
    \,d\bm{x}_{t-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since $\bm{x}_{t-1}$ and the independent process noise $\bm{q}_t$ are Gaussian, and $\bm{x}_t$ is an affine transformation of them, the predictive distribution is also Gaussian:
\[
    p(
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t}
    )
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1},
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \right).
\]

Its mean is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    [
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t}
    ]
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Definition of predictive mean}
    \\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    [
        \mathbf{F}_t\bm{x}_{t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t
        +
        \bm{q}_t
    ]
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{State transition model}
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{F}_t
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_{t-1}]
    +
    \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t
    +
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{q}_t]
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Linearity of expectation}
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{F}_t
    \bm{\mu}_{t-1\mid t-1}
    +
    \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Zero-mean process noise}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Its covariance is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t-1},
        \bm{u}_{1:t}
    \right)
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Definition of predictive covariance}
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \mathbf{F}_t\bm{x}_{t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t
        +
        \bm{q}_t
    \right)
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{State transition model}
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \mathbf{F}_t\bm{x}_{t-1}
        +
        \bm{q}_t
    \right)
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Deterministic control input}
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \mathbf{F}_t\bm{x}_{t-1}
    \right)
    +
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{q}_t)
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Independence of state and process noise}
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{F}_t
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_{t-1})
    \mathbf{F}_t^\top
    +
    \mathbf{Q}_t
    &&\leftarrow
    \text{Covariance under linear transformation}
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{F}_t
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1\mid t-1}
    \mathbf{F}_t^\top
    +
    \mathbf{Q}_t.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, the prediction step of the Kalman filter is
\[
    {
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
        =
        \mathbf{F}_t
        \bm{\mu}_{t-1\mid t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{B}_t\bm{u}_t
    }
\]
and
\[
    {
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        =
        \mathbf{F}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t-1\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{F}_t^\top
        +
        \mathbf{Q}_t
    }.
\]

\subsection{Correction Step}

Suppose that the observation model is also linear:
\[
    \bm{z}_t
    =
    \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t
    +
    \bm{r}_t,
    \qquad
    \bm{r}_t
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{R}_t),
    \qquad
    t\in\{1,\dots,T\},
\]
where the observation noise $\bm{r}_t$ is independent of the state $\bm{x}_t$. Thus,
\[
    p(\bm{z}_t\mid\bm{x}_t)
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t,
        \mathbf{R}_t
    \right).
\]

In the following correction-step derivation, all expectations, covariances, and distributions involving $\bm{x}_t$ and $\bm{z}_t$ are implicitly conditioned on the information available before observing $\bm{z}_t$, namely
\[
    (\bm{z}_{1:t-1},\bm{u}_{1:t}),
\]
and this conditioning is suppressed when no ambiguity arises.

We first show that the predicted state $\bm{x}_t$ and the observation $\bm{z}_t$ are jointly Gaussian. From the observation model,
\[
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{x}_t\\
        \bm{z}_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{x}_t\\
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t+\bm{r}_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{I} & \mathbf{0}\\
        \mathbf{H}_t & \mathbf{I}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{x}_t\\
        \bm{r}_t
    \end{bmatrix}.
\]

Before observing $\bm{z}_t$,
\[
    \bm{x}_t
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1},
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \right),
\]
and
\[
    \bm{r}_t
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \bm{0},
        \mathbf{R}_t
    \right).
\]

Since $\bm{x}_t$ and $\bm{r}_t$ are independent Gaussian random vectors,
\[
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{x}_t\\
        \bm{r}_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}\\
            \bm{0}
        \end{bmatrix},
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1} & \mathbf{0}\\
            \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}_t
        \end{bmatrix}
    \right).
\]

Because a linear transformation of a Gaussian random vector remains Gaussian, $\bm{x}_t$ and $\bm{z}_t$ are jointly Gaussian.

The predicted-state mean is
\[
    \bm{\mu}_{\bm{x}t}
    =
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_t]
    =
    \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}.
\]

The predicted observation mean is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{\mu}_{\bm{z}t}
    &=
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{z}_t]
    \\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t+\bm{r}_t
    \right]
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{H}_t
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_t]
    +
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{r}_t]
    \\
    &=
    {
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
    }.
\end{aligned}
\]

The predicted-state covariance is
\[
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}t}
    =
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t)
    =
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}.
\]

The cross-covariance between the state and the observation is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}\bm{z}t}
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t,\bm{z}_t)
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \bm{x}_t,
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t+\bm{r}_t
    \right)
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \bm{x}_t,
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t
    \right)
    +
    \underbrace{
        \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t,\bm{r}_t)
    }_{=\mathbf{0}}
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t)
    \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    \\
    &=
    {
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    }.
\end{aligned}
\]

Similarly,
\[
    {
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\bm{z}\bm{x}t}
        =
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    }.
\]

Finally, the observation covariance is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{\bm{z}\bm{z}t}
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{z}_t)
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}
    \left(
        \mathbf{H}_t\bm{x}_t+\bm{r}_t
    \right)
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{H}_t
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t)
    \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    +
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{r}_t)
    \\
    &=
    {
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
        +
        \mathbf{R}_t
    }.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,
\[
    {
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \bm{x}_t\\
        \bm{z}_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}\\
            \mathbf{H}_t\bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
        \end{bmatrix},
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
            &
            \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}\mathbf{H}_t^\top
            \\
            \mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
            &
            \mathbf{H}_t
            \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
            \mathbf{H}_t^\top
            +
            \mathbf{R}_t
        \end{bmatrix}
    \right).
    }
\]

The difference between the actual observation and its predicted mean is called the \emph{innovation}:
\[
    {
        \bm{\nu}_t
        :=
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbb{E}[\bm{z}_t]
        =
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
    }.
\]

Before observing $\bm{z}_t$, the innovation has zero mean:
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{\nu}_t]
    &=
    \mathbb{E}
    \left[
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbb{E}[\bm{z}_t]
    \right]
    \\
    &=
    \bm{0}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Its covariance is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{\nu}_t)
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{z}_t)
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{H}_t
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    +
    \mathbf{R}_t.
\end{aligned}
\]

We define the \emph{innovation covariance}
\[
    {
        \mathbf{S}_t
        :=
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
        +
        \mathbf{R}_t
    }.
\]

The two terms in $\mathbf{S}_t$ have distinct interpretations:
\[
    \underbrace{
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    }_{\text{uncertainty in the predicted observation}}
    +
    \underbrace{
        \mathbf{R}_t
    }_{\text{observation noise}}.
\]

Since $\bm{x}_t$ and $\bm{z}_t$ are jointly Gaussian, conditioning on the newly observed $\bm{z}_t$ gives a Gaussian posterior:
\[
    p(
        \bm{x}_t
        \mid
        \bm{z}_{1:t},
        \bm{u}_{1:t}
    )
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t},
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t}
    \right).
\]

For jointly Gaussian random vectors, the conditional mean is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t}
    &=
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_t\mid\bm{z}_t]
    \\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}[\bm{x}_t]
    +
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t,\bm{z}_t)
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{z}_t)^{-1}
    \left(
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbb{E}[\bm{z}_t]
    \right)
    \\
    &=
    \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
    +
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    \mathbf{S}_t^{-1}
    \left(
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
    \right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Define the \emph{Kalman gain}
\[
    {
        \mathbf{K}_t
        :=
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
        \mathbf{S}_t^{-1}
    }.
\]

The posterior mean can therefore be written compactly as
\[
    {
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t}
        =
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{K}_t
        \bm{\nu}_t
    }.
\]

Thus, the new observation corrects the predicted mean by an amount proportional to the innovation $\bm{\nu}_t$, while the Kalman gain $\mathbf{K}_t$ determines how strongly the observation should influence the estimate.

For jointly Gaussian random vectors, the conditional covariance is
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t}
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t\mid\bm{z}_t)
    \\
    &=
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t)
    -
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{x}_t,\bm{z}_t)
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{z}_t)^{-1}
    \operatorname{Cov}(\bm{z}_t,\bm{x}_t)
    \\
    &=
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    -
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \mathbf{H}_t^\top
    \mathbf{S}_t^{-1}
    \mathbf{H}_t
    \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    \\
    &=
    {
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        -
        \mathbf{K}_t
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    }.
\end{aligned}
\]

Equivalently,
\[
    {
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t}
        =
        \left(
            \mathbf{I}
            -
            \mathbf{K}_t\mathbf{H}_t
        \right)
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
    }.
\]

Therefore, the correction step of the Kalman filter can be summarized as
\[
    {
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm{\nu}_t
        &=
        \bm{z}_t
        -
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1},
        \\
        \mathbf{S}_t
        &=
        \mathbf{H}_t
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
        +
        \mathbf{R}_t,
        \\
        \mathbf{K}_t
        &=
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}
        \mathbf{H}_t^\top
        \mathbf{S}_t^{-1},
        \\
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t}
        &=
        \bm{\mu}_{t\mid t-1}
        +
        \mathbf{K}_t\bm{\nu}_t,
        \\
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t}
        &=
        \left(
            \mathbf{I}
            -
            \mathbf{K}_t\mathbf{H}_t
        \right)
        \mathbf{\Sigma}_{t\mid t-1}.
    \end{aligned}
    }
\]

Hence, the Kalman filter alternates between two operations:
\[
    {
        \text{prediction}
        \quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{correction}
    }.
\]

The prediction step propagates the previous posterior distribution through the linear-Gaussian state transition model, while the correction step combines the resulting predictive distribution with the new observation. Because Gaussian distributions are closed under linear transformations and Gaussian conditioning, both the predictive and posterior state distributions remain Gaussian at every time step.
